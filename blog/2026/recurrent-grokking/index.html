On Serial Depth and Monitorability in Looped Transformers, Part 1

Blog post for part 1 of a 2-part study on the computation that happens before a token is emitted in looped transformers, motivating future work in studying the impact that an increased serial depth could have on monitorability.

tl;dr: Chain-of-thought monitoring is built on the assumption that models have too little computation per token to reason without writing it down, and looped transformers are built to relax that exactly limit. This post is part 1 of a 2-part study on intra-token computation in looped transformers. I follow the training recipe from Nanda et al. (2023) to compare a one-layer Vaswani et al. transformer baseline, a simplified looped model based on the universal transformer, and a Huginn variant on the task of $n$-hop modular addition base $113$. The looped models that grokked, I observe, do not learn iterative algorithms in this problem setting. Instead, these models abuse a parallel shortcut by attending to each input operand evenly from the = token, with 2 of 3 examined models reading the result from a ‘global clock’. Despite their shared outward behavior, the models utilize their loops in varying ways, achieving similar task generalization performance across strategies. I additionally study the periodic training collapses I experienced in training recurrent grokkers under the Nanda et al. recipe, and propose a simple fix with standard learning rate/weight decay scheduling.

This work motivates part 2, a contrasting study in a problem setting where there exists no 1-pass shortcut, and additionally motivates further exploration into the development of monitoring tools for understanding both the intra- and inter-token computation in looped transformers. [code]

(A note from me: Due to compute limitations, most experiments were run on a single seed and confirmed on a second one. I report this accordingly with each result.)

Foreword: An Essay On Safety and Looped Transformers

In the current landscape of AI safety, monitoring the chain of thought (CoT) is the “primary bet” for detecting and preventing agentic misalignment. The unfortunate tension, as Jakub Pachocki writes, is that “evaluations indicate our ability to rely on CoT monitoring is progressively diminishing.” Despite this, the GPT-6 family system card outlines a safety system built around a pipeline that “reviews the agent’s chain of thought alongside its actions.”

The argument for CoT monitoring in the first place is somewhat architectural. We assume the model offloads the necessary computation to produce an answer to its reasoning tokens because it has a fixed computation budget (i.e., an opaque serial depth) per forward pass. This assumption is pretty strong, yet it is the foundation for the belief that the CoT carries the true underlying thoughts of the model in the first place.

The question is, if an increase in serial depth corresponds to increased violation of this assumption, how big of a threat to monitorability would that be? As a model increases in per-token computation capacity, the CoT becomes less and less necessary. My concern with this direction is that this liberation from the CoT as a necessary component could bring with it a higher risk of CoT obfuscationWherein, the CoT stops being a direct reflection of the model's internal computation. in subsequent model generations. However, this concern is not new to the field.

This core CoT monitorability position paper, written by researchers from across the frontier labs and academia, identifies CoT monitoring as a fragile opportunity in its title, and warns the field about making research decisions that pressure the model to obfuscate the CoT in its abstract. In fact, OpenAI themselves have even published work on the risks of obfuscation. Despite the attention on the topic of monitorability and the importance of avoiding obfuscation, recent work has shown models are capable of obfuscating the CoT through output supervision, training on documents about monitoring, and in some cases even pure prompt pressure.

Looped transformers introduce a distinct threat to CoT monitorability as they seek to alleviate the serial depth limitation of existing transformer architectures. I borrow a taxonomy from user Skye on LessWrong, who was in the lab responsible for Ouro. They break down monitorability concerns into those relating to intra-token computation (or “computation within a single timestep (before the model commits to a token)”), and those relating to inter-token computation (or “computation from one timestep to the next”).

Skye argues that inter-token computation is the bigger danger in the long term. While I agree that the increased potential for inter-token computation as a vector for obfuscation is the larger risk in the limit, I don’t think a larger (and even variable) serial depth is inherently safe purely because it remains bounded. Put simply, a monitor fails when a model can do both the intra-token computation to support a hidden line of reasoning and control its CoT enough to obfuscate it. Neel Nanda notes that these appear to be separate abilities: “controllability seems very dependent on details of post-training, while no CoT reasoning is largely determined by the pretrain.” Since looping is an architectural and thus a pretraining decision, it has the potential to move the no-CoT axis directly, independent of post-training. So I think the potential for CoT obfuscation along both axes should be studied.

Studying the impact of recurrence is also, in many ways, plain timely. The last year and a half or so has seen the publication of a substantial number of open LoopLMsTerm describing language models built around these recurrent transformers, from the Ouro paper. including Huginn, the aforementioned Ouro, Nanbeige, and ParcaeFor even more evidence that the field is considering LoopLMs as a way forward, the Parcae paper even includes their own scaling laws for looping.. Beyond the open releases, there has been much discussion of looped transformers making their way into frontier labs. The case for Astra being a monitorability concern is real, and as Neel Nanda remarks, Astra can do a “concerning amount” with no CoT.

To be fair, OpenAI attributes Astra’s lower CoT monitorability to capability rather than architecture, Astra’s looped design is still only reported rather than confirmed, and skeptics read the decline as a stronger model writing shorter traces. But Astra’s no-CoT ability doesn’t look like plain capability: in the figure above, it sits well off the trend line the other models share. And my argument doesn’t depend on Astra being looped: it applies to any model that fits more serial computation into a single token, and looping is just the most direct way to get there.

Beyond the clear jump in potential for intra-token computation, the CoT traces that models like Astra do produce are becoming increasingly controllable. Work done as recently as March of this year claims there exists little to no controllability over CoT in frontier language models, using the same evaluation suite (CoT-Control) that OpenAI reported in their official Astra safety overview in early September. According to that overview, Astra has a 60.9% controllability rate. This is a massive jump from the also-reported 16.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 1.7% for GPT-5.5 Thinking.These rates are for CoT traces between 750 and 1,250 tokens long. Possibly the most damning detail is that this behavior shows up unprompted. The same safety overview reports that “under simple monitoring-awareness (without being told to evade the monitor), [Astra] spontaneously generates substantially fewer chain-of-thought tokens,” and that in adversarial evaluations it “can sometimes evade our internal monitors when asked to perform certain sabotage tasks.” This constitutes a plain, concrete example of the potential threat vectors future monitoring tools will have to consider.

My claim is this: when the distribution of emitted tokens is highly controllable and enough serial computation fits within a single forward pass, a monitor built around reading from those emitted tokens is reading what the model chose to show it. Monitoring tools for the next generation of latent reasoners must touch the computation itself.

So… what does that look like?

I have a few ideas on where we can start looking. To isolate one direction, the remark that the potential for intra-token computation appears determined by pre-training seems to suggest that aligning that intra-token computation would have to involve some intervention performed in the pre-training stage. As it currently stands, we don’t have many of those.For more on the need for pre-training-based methods for alignment, see this talk.

However, we should take a step back. Before we can think about methods for addressing these new threats to monitorability, we should first understand how an increase in serial depth changes what happens inside a single token. Checking anything about a loop’s computation, whether through a monitor or the model’s own account of it, needs ground truth for what the loop actually computes in these architectures. In this project, I start with looped models trained on a task with a known algorithm in standard transformers: the ‘clock’ algorithm for modular addition.

One caveat shapes the rest of this post and the results: addition mod $p$ is commutative, so a chain of additions can be solved in a single pass by averaging the operands. This post asks what a loop does with a task it doesn’t need to loop for; part 2 removes the shortcut.
