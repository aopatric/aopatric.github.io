On Serial Depth and Monitorability in Looped Transformers, Part 1

Blog post for part 1 of a 2-part study on the computation that happens before a token is emitted in looped transformers, motivating future work in studying the impact that an increased serial depth could have on monitorability.

tl;dr: Part 1 of a 2-part study on intra-token computation in looped transformers. I follow the training recipe from Nanda et al. (2023) to compare a one-layer Vaswani et al. transformer baseline, a simplified looped model based on the universal transformer, and a Huginn variant on the task of $n$-hop modular addition base $113$. I find that in this task the the looped models do not learn iterative algorithms and instead abuse a 1-pass shortcut while utilizing their loops in varying ways, achieving similar task generalization performance across strategies. I additionally study the periodic training collapses I experienced in training recurrent grokkers under the Nanda et al. recipe, and propose a simple fix with standard learning rate/weight decay scheduling. This work motivates part 2, a contrasting study in a problem setting where there exists no 1-pass shortcut, and additionally motivates further exploration into the development of monitoring tools for understanding both the intra- and inter-token computation in looped transformers. foreword: an essay on safety and looped transformers

In the current landscape of production AI safety, monitoring the chain of thought (CoT) is the most relied-upon method for detecting agentic misalignment. The unfortunate tension, as Jakub Pachocki writes, is that “evaluations indicate our ability to rely on CoT monitoring is progressively diminishing.” Despite this, the GPT-6 family system card outlines a safety system built around a pipeline that “reviews the agent’s chain of thought alongside its actions.”

The argument for CoT monitoring in the first place is somewhat architectural. We assume the model offloads computation to its reasoning tokens because it has a fixed computation budget (i.e., an opaque serial depth). However, even the CoT monitorability position paper identifies it as a fragile opportunity in its title, and warns the field about making research decisions that pressure the model to obfuscate the CoT in its abstract. OpenAI themselves have even published work on the risks of obfuscation.

Nevertheless, recent work has shown models are capable of obfuscating the CoT through output supervision, training on documents about monitoring, and in some cases even pure prompt pressure.

Looped transformers introduce a distinct threat to CoT monitorability as they seek to alleviate the serial depth limitation of existing transformer architectures. I borrow a taxonomy from user Skye on LessWrong, who was in the lab responsible for Ouro. They break down monitorability concerns into those relating to intra-token computation (or “computation within a single timestep (before the model commits to a token)”), and those relating to inter-token computation (or “computation from one timestep to the next”).

Skye argues that inter-token computation is the bigger danger in the long term. I agree that the increased potential for inter-token computation is the larger risk in the limit, but I don’t think a larger (and even variable) serial depth is inherently safe just because it’s bounded. I’d argue that as serial depth grows, the CoT becomes more of a projection of the model’s internal state and less a direct record of its reasoning process. Put simply, a monitor fails when a model can do both the intra-token computation to support hidden reasoning and control its CoT enough to obfuscate it. Neel Nanda notes that these are separate abilities: “controllability seems very dependent on details of post-training, while no CoT reasoning is largely determined by the pretrain.” Since looping is an architectural and thus a pretraining decision, it has the potential to move the no-CoT axis directly, independent of post-training. So I think the potential for CoT obfuscation along both axes should be studied.

Studying the impact of recurrence in particular on monitorability is also plainly timely. The past two years have seen the publication of a substantial number of open LoopLMs including Huginn, the aforementioned Ouro, Nanbeige, and ParcaeFor even more evidence that the field is considering LoopLMs as a way forward, the Parcae paper even includes their own scaling laws for looping.. Beyond the open releases, there has been much discussion of looped transformers making their way into frontier labs. The case for Astra being a monitorability concern is real, and as Neel Nanda remarks, Astra can do a “concerning amount” with no CoT.

Beyond intra-token computation, the CoT that models like Astra do produce is becoming increasingly controllable. Work done as recently as March of this year claims little controllability over CoT in frontier language models, using the same evaluation suite (CoT-Control) that OpenAI reports in the Astra safety overview. On it, Astra has a self-reported 60.9% controllability rate, a far cry from 16.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 1.7% for GPT-5.5 Thinking.These rates are for CoTs between 750 and 1,250 tokens long. This control already shows up unprompted: the same safety overview reports that “under simple monitoring-awareness (without being told to evade the monitor), [Astra] spontaneously generates substantially fewer chain-of-thought tokens,” and that in adversarial evaluations it “can sometimes evade our internal monitors when asked to perform certain sabotage tasks.”

My claim is this: when the distribution of emitted tokens is highly controllable and enough serial computation fits within a single forward pass, a monitor built around reading from those emitted tokens is reading what the model chose to show it. Monitoring tools for the next generation of latent reasoners must touch the computation itself.

This two-part project aims to isolate the intra-token computation in looped transformers in order to study the degree to which it could affect monitorability.
