Serial Depth, Monitorability, and Interpretability in Looped Transformers (Essay)

Accompanying essay motivating my related work (see the part 1 write-up here) focusing on the internals of looped transformers in the broader context of AI safety. Opinions are my own.

tl;dr: CoT monitoring is both a heavily relied-on and fragile tool. The case for CoT monitoring leans on an architectural assumption: models have too little per-token computation power to reason without verbalizing their thoughts. Looped transformers (those that run a core set of layers for multiple iterations) are built to relax this exact constraint. I argue this poses concern for our ability to reasonably monitor the behavior of these models in the future, draw on empirical evidence to support the argument, and address counterarguments. I conclude by arguing for monitoring tools that observe what the model cannot control, rather than what the model chose to show it.

Bad Bets

In the current landscape of AI safety, monitoring the chain of thought (CoT) is the “primary bet” for detecting and preventing agentic misalignment. The unfortunate contradiction, as Jakub Pachocki writes in his blog post “An Alien Mind”, is that “evaluations indicate our ability to rely on CoT monitoring is progressively diminishing.” Despite this, the GPT-6 family system card outlines a safety system built around a central pipeline that “reviews the agent’s chain of thought alongside its actions.”

The argument for CoT monitoring in the first place is somewhat architectural. We assume the model offloads the necessary computation to produce an answer to its reasoning tokens because it has a fixed computation budget (i.e., serial depth) per forward pass. This assumption is built on empirical observation with existing models, yet it is central to our belief that the CoT carries the true thoughts of a model in the first place.

My question is this: if an increase in serial depth means a potential violation of this assumption, how big of an impact could that have on our ability to monitor our models? As a model increases in per-token computation capacity, it is empirically observed that the CoT becomes less and less necessary for sequential computation. My concern with this direction is that this independence from the CoT could bring with it a higher risk of CoT obfuscationWherein, the CoT is altered to hide misaligned intent and/or action. in subsequent model generations. However, this concern is not new to the field.

This core CoT monitorability position paper, written by researchers from across the frontier labs and academia, identifies CoT monitoring as a fragile opportunity in its title, and warns the field about making research decisions that pressure the model to obfuscate the CoT in its abstract. In fact, OpenAI themselves have even published work on the risks of obfuscation. Despite the attention on the topic of monitorability and the importance of avoiding obfuscation, recent work has shown models are capable of obfuscating the CoT through output supervision, training on documents about monitoring, and in some cases even pure prompt pressure.

Threat Vectors and the Limits of Boundedness

My stance is that looped transformers introduce a distinct threat to CoT monitorability as they seek to directly alleviate the serial depth limitation of existing transformer architectures. At a literal level, a looped transformer just runs a core block several times per token. Thus the amount of computation we can fit into a token is governed by loop iterations, not layer count. While a standard transformer is inherently limited in depth, a looped transformer can potentially trade additional compute for extra serial depth. The idea is that this tradeoff could enable additional reasoning within each token and increase the potential for CoT obfuscation and other undesirable outcomes.

To be more precise about where computation happens, I borrow a taxonomy from user Skye on LessWrong, who was in the lab responsible for Ouro. They break down monitorability concerns into those relating to intra-token computation (or “computation within a single timestep (before the model commits to a token)”), and those relating to inter-token computation (or “computation from one timestep to the next”).

Skye makes a natural objection to my point, being that a model’s serial depth is bounded, however much looping increases it. The core of their argument is that inter-token computation poses the bigger danger in the long term, since the masked inter-token computation is the most direct pathway to obfuscating the CoT. While I mostly agree with this point, I’m a bit hesitant to accept the implication that scaling the serial depth ceiling without further safeguards in place is inherently a non-issue purely because depth remains finite.

The issue is that bounded or not, the extra depth looping buys relaxes the exact same serial depth constraint that CoT monitoring leans onQuick aside: there's some intuition to be gained for the benefit of increased depth by breaking down classic results from deep learning theory. The universal approximation theorem tells us that a wide enough ReLU net with one hidden layer can produce an $\epsilon$-close approximation of any continuous function on a bounded domain, out of piecewise linear components. A classic follow-up result is the manner in which the amount of expressible piecewise linear components grows with width and depth. While expressivity grows polynomially in width, it grows exponentially in depth. That exponential is a ceiling on what a network can express, though, not necessarily what emerges in practice. Empirically, networks tend to realize far fewer piecewise linear components, with a less than exponential gain from depth. Still, depth was always an intriguing scaling axis; the concern is that looping could add arbitrary depth without extra parameters.. Recent exploration suggests that relaxing the serial depth constraint can translate to a real capability increase. The cited work shows that increasing serial depth even logarithmically with the input length lets a transformer solve reasoning tasks that complexity theory states fixed-depth transformers cannot, and that scaling depth is actually more efficient than scaling the number of CoT steps. To hammer this point home: Saunshi et al. show that a looped transformer has the ability to effectively simulate $T$ steps of CoT reasoning output with $T$ loops, generating “latent thoughts” that never appear as output tokens themselves.

Put simply, a monitor fails when a model has both the no-CoT ability to carry a hidden line of reasoning and enough control over its CoT to obfuscate it. In Skye’s terms, the first is a question of intra-token computation, the work done before each token is committed, while the second governs what the inter-token channel, the CoT itself, actually shows. Neel Nanda notes that these appear to be separate abilities: “controllability seems very dependent on details of post-training, while no CoT reasoning is largely determined by the pretrain.” Since looping is an architectural and thus a pretraining decision, it has the potential to move the no-CoT axis directly, independent of post-training. So I think the potential for CoT obfuscation along both axes, no-CoT ability and controllability, should be studied.

Star Signs and Mind Reading

So we know there is some evidence in the literature supporting the idea that loops could bring about increased hidden reasoning capability. But how relevant is looping today? Well, the last year and a half or so has seen the publication of a substantial number of open LoopLMsTerm describing language models built around looped transformers, from the Ouro paper. including Huginn, the aforementioned Ouro, Nanbeige, and ParcaeFor some more evidence that looping is viewed as an additional axis for scaling, the Parcae paper even includes its own scaling laws for looping.. Beyond the open releases, there has been discussion of looped transformers making their way into frontier labs. Much of this concern has focused on GPT-6 Astra in particular. According to Neel Nanda, Astra can do a “concerning amount” with no CoT.

Earlier I cited LatentMathBench, a benchmark which disables the CoT and asks a model to work through a sequence of arithmetic assignments, measuring how many steps it can handle while still answering correctly half the time. On this measure, Astra’s horizon is roughly 4× that of the next best OpenAI model, GPT-5.6 Sol, and 7× that of GPT-4. The size of this jump is hard to ignore.

Beyond the serial depth jump, the CoT traces that models like Astra do produce are becoming increasingly controllable. Work done as recently as March of this year found there existed little to no controllability over CoT in frontier language models, using the same evaluation suite (CoT-Control) that OpenAI reported in their official Astra safety overview in early September. According to that September overview, Astra has a 60.9% controllability rate. This is a massive jump from the also-reported 16.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 1.7% for GPT-5.5 Thinking.These rates are for CoT traces between 750 and 1,250 tokens long. Possibly the most damning detail is that some of this behavior shows up unprompted: the same safety overview reports that “under simple monitoring-awareness (without being told to evade the monitor), [Astra] spontaneously generates substantially fewer chain-of-thought tokens.” In adversarial evaluations, the overview adds, it “can sometimes evade our internal monitors when asked to perform certain sabotage tasks.”

To be fair, OpenAI has stated that it attributes Astra’s increased CoT controllability to a capability increase rather than an architectural difference. Astra’s looped design is still only reported rather than confirmed, and some read the decline in CoT trace length generated as a stronger model writing shorter traces. But a stronger model needing less CoT is exactly the concern: the shorter the trace, the less of the model’s reasoning it carries. My argument, however, does not depend on the increase coming from architectural changes. I simply view architectural changes as the easiest path to a significant jump in serial depth. The reason that I focus on architecture in particular over plain capability is the figure presented above. Astra sits well off the trend line the other models share.

Perhaps the most interesting counterargument I came across was from the commenters on R. Arike’s LessWrong post on a similar topic, focused on Astra. The comments section includes some valuable discussion, including the argument that since a loop’s intermediate states could be decodable, looping might actually help with monitoring, and others even reported decoding them in open LoopLMs like Huginn and Ouro. As Arike notes in a reply, though, this is “pretty similar to using the logit lens,” and “substantially worse than proper CoT, but better than nothing.” I think this is a direction worth studying more deeply, but not one I’d take as reassurance, and certainly not an end-all-be-all solution. As it stands, we don’t have many strongly reliable tools for this kind of latent mind reading, at the token level or the CoT level. However, this doesn’t have to be the case!

Taking a Deeper Look

My claim is this: when the distribution of emitted tokens is highly controllable and enough serial computation fits within a single forward pass, a monitor built around reading from those emitted tokens is reading what the model chose to show it. Reliable monitoring tools for the next generation of latent reasoners must touch the computation itself.

So… what does that look like?

At the moment, I’m honestly unsure if there’s a single answer out there. Worthwhile tools for this job could take any number of shapes.

Some could even act before the model exists at all, like in the data. Data-first approaches to this are already out there, and show potential for more robust alignment of downstream models. These are grounded in the finding that safeguards added after training, unlearning included, are easily undone by fine-tuning, while filtering the pretraining data holds up much better against tampering.

Another potential direction is algorithmic improvement at the pre-training stage. The motivation for this comes from the same Neel Nanda post describing the separation between CoT controllability and No-CoT reasoning ability. If we care about ensuring the model is behaving as intended without reliance on the CoT, then maybe it makes sense to ensure the stages of training most impactful on No-CoT computation are well-aligned, as well. As it currently stands, we don’t have many methods for doing so at a method level.

Others could attempt to monitor the computation itself, akin to the “mind reader” imagery from earlier. I think of this idea (in the best of all possible worlds) as upgrading from user space to kernel access: a model might struggle to keep things hidden the deeper and deeper we dig. However, this approach and others like it tell a story as old as the field itself: algorithmic pressure on the interpretability signals is a dangerous game.

Whatever shape the winning tool might take in the coming years, the goal is the same: monitorability. Mechanistic interpretability itself should be viewed as a means here, not the end. What really matters is whether the tool we choose still tells us something true about what the model is doing, even when the model would rather it didn’t. We should be satisfied by reliability of any mechanistic or otherwise internal tools to at least the same degree as we are by the CoT.

To this end, tools for disentangling latent thoughts face the same optimization pressure concerns that currently threaten the CoT. Bailey et al. show that the model activations themselves can be modified through the prompt itself or through fine-tuning to evade internal probes and monitoring tools, all the while allowing the undesirable behavior to continue. The lesson we’re seemingly learning from CoT monitoring carries over: if we choose to read from some oracle inside the model, we should think twice about putting any optimization pressure on it.

Until now, the CoT has been a reliable tool because models think out loud by necessity. Increasing serial computation depth is one of the most direct ways that necessity could be broken. If it is, the monitors worth building are those that read what the model cannot control, rather than what it chose to show them. Whether such a lens exists that can withstand optimization pressure is perhaps a bigger question.
